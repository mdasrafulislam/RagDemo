Traditional IRA

Contributions are pre-tax or tax-deductible, reducing taxable income in the year they are made. Growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

The annual contribution limit is 7,000 dollars, plus a 1,000 dollar catch-up contribution for account holders aged 50 and over. The deduction phases out at higher income levels when the account holder or their spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan.

Withdrawals before age 59 and a half generally carry a 10 percent early withdrawal penalty on top of income tax. Required minimum distributions must begin at age 73.


401(k) and 403(b) Plans

Both are employer-sponsored. A 401(k) is offered by private sector employers, while a 403(b) is offered by public schools, universities, and tax-exempt organizations under section 501(c)(3). They otherwise operate on similar principles.

The elective deferral limit is 23,500 dollars. Participants aged 50 and over may add a 7,500 dollar catch-up contribution. Under SECURE 2.0, participants aged 60 through 63 receive an enhanced catch-up of 11,250 dollars in place of the standard amount.

Many employers match contributions. Employee deferrals are always 100 percent vested immediately, but employer contributions may be subject to a vesting schedule requiring a period of service before the participant owns them.

Both plans come in a traditional pre-tax version and a Roth after-tax version. Traditional contributions reduce taxable income now and are taxed on withdrawal. Roth contributions give no deduction now, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are entirely tax-free.


SEP IRA and SIMPLE IRA

Both serve self-employed individuals and small business owners.

A SEP IRA is funded entirely by the employer. Employees cannot make salary deferrals into it. The employer may contribute up to 25 percent of compensation and must use the same percentage for every eligible employee.

A SIMPLE IRA is available to employers with 100 or fewer employees. Employees may defer up to 16,500 dollars, with a 3,500 dollar catch-up at age 50 and over. The employer must either match deferrals up to 3 percent of compensation or make a 2 percent non-elective contribution for all eligible employees. Withdrawals taken within the first two years of participation carry a 25 percent penalty instead of the usual 10 percent.


Health Savings Account

An HSA requires enrollment in a qualifying high-deductible health plan. It offers a triple tax advantage: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

The contribution limit is 4,300 dollars for self-only coverage and 8,550 dollars for family coverage, with a 1,000 dollar catch-up from age 55. There is no use-it-or-lose-it rule, and balances roll over indefinitely.

Before age 65, non-medical withdrawals incur a 20 percent penalty plus income tax. From age 65 onward, non-medical withdrawals are permitted without the penalty and are taxed as ordinary income, which makes the account function much like a Traditional IRA. HSAs are not subject to required minimum distributions.
